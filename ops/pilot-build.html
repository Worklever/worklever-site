Scene 1 — HOOK

Zapier and Make can build the exact same automation and bill you completely differently for it. Here is the part almost every comparison gets backwards. Make counts more things than Zapier does. It charges for the trigger, and it charges every time it checks — even when there is nothing there. That sounds like the expensive one. But on published entry pricing, a single Make credit costs roughly one forty-fourth of a single Zapier task. More units, far cheaper units. Which one actually wins depends entirely on the shape of your workflow, and you can work it out in about ten minutes.

Scene 4 — MECHANIC — ZAPIER

Zapier's unit is the task. In Zapier's own words: a task is any successful action that runs in Zapier — only successful actions count toward your task usage. Two words there are doing heavy lifting. Successful — failed actions are not counted. And action — the thing that starts your workflow is a trigger, not an action, so it does not count at all. Zapier's help documentation is blunt about it: Zap triggers never use tasks. Polling for new data is free too. So Zapier can check for new orders all day long and charge you nothing for looking.

Scene 7 — MECHANIC — MAKE

And there is a multiplier, though it is narrower than most people think. Make's documentation says action modules — the add, update and delete steps — use as many credits as runs needed to process all bundles: one credit per input bundle processed. In plain English, a step that writes ten rows costs ten credits, not one. Iterators work the same way: one credit to split an array, plus one credit for every bundle sent downstream. This is the single biggest driver of surprise bills on Make, and it has nothing to do with the price of the plan. It is entirely about how many bundles each step has to process.

Scene 11 — THE CORRECTION

Here is where most comparisons go wrong, including one we nearly published. It is tempting to hear that Make bills for empty checks and conclude that Make is the expensive one. That gets the causation backwards. Make's meter running faster is a headwind against Make. It is the thing working in Zapier's favour. Make's advantage comes from somewhere else entirely — the price of the unit. So the real question is a race. Make counts more units. Make's units cost less. Which effect is bigger for your workflow?

Scene 12 — METHOD

So here is the method. Four numbers, and you can get all four from looking at one workflow. Number one: R, the runs per month that actually do something — real orders, real form fills. Number two: A, the actions per run that reach out and touch another app. Number three: C, the checks per month — if you poll every fifteen minutes that is about two thousand nine hundred; if your trigger is event-driven, C is just R. Number four: B, the extra bundles, meaning items a module processes beyond the first.

VISUAL: Four numbered cards flip in and lock into a row, each with a plain-English one-line definition and a small worked example. Deliberately low-tech, worksheet aesthetic — this is the takeaway the viewer screenshots. Ensure the 15-minute polling maths (96/day × 30) is visible on card three.

ON-SCREEN TEXT (add verbatim as Fliki text layers): THE 4 NUMBERS R = real runs / month A = actions per run C = checks / month (poll freq × 30 days) B = extra bundles per run

Scene 13 — METHOD

Two formulas. Zapier tasks equal R times A — because the trigger and the logic are free. Make credits equal C, plus R times A, plus B — because the checks count, the trigger counts, and the bundles multiply. Then multiply each by the rate you read off each pricing slider at your own volume. And the crossover rule, which is the whole video in one line: Zapier only wins on the meter when Make's unit count exceeds Zapier's by more than the price ratio. At entry tiers, that means more than forty-four times.

Scene 18 — LIMITS

And the honest part: what a cost model cannot tell you. It cannot tell you which platform reliably connects to the specific apps your business runs on. It cannot price the hours you will lose to a tool that does not fit how you think. It cannot measure error recovery when a workflow breaks at two in the morning, or how quickly support answers. None of that shows up on a pricing page, and for most small businesses those factors decide the outcome long before the meter does.
