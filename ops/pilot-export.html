Scene 1 — hook

Zapier and Make can build the exact same automation and bill you completely differently for it. Here is the part almost every comparison gets backwards. Make counts more things than Zapier does. It charges for the trigger, and it charges every time it checks — even when there is nothing there. That sounds like the expensive one. But on published entry pricing, a single Make credit costs roughly one forty-fourth of a single Zapier task. More units, far cheaper units. Which one actually wins depends entirely on the shape of your workflow, and you can work it out in about ten minutes.

Scene 2 — methodology

One note on where this comes from, because it matters. Nobody here signed up, ran a trial, or tested either product. Every price and every definition you are about to see is quoted from Zapier's and Make's own pricing pages and official help docs, retrieved on the twenty-eighth of July, twenty twenty-six. Anything that is our own arithmetic gets labelled MODELLED on screen, so you always know which is which. Prices move. Treat this as a method you can re-run, not a permanent answer.

Scene 3 — Zapier task (1.00×)

Zapier's unit is the task. In Zapier's own words: a task is any successful action that runs in Zapier — only successful actions count toward your task usage. Successful — failed actions are not counted. And action — the thing that starts your workflow is a trigger, not an action, so it does not count at all. Zapier's help documentation is blunt: Zap triggers never use tasks, and polling for new data is free. Zapier can check for new orders all day long and charge you nothing for looking.

Scene 4 — Zapier free steps

There is a second piece people miss, and it quietly matters more than the headline price. Zapier's own documentation says filter steps, path steps and Formatter steps are free. Its pricing page adds that built-in data tools do not count either. So the logic that makes a workflow smart — the branching, the if-this-then-that, the date reformatting — sits outside the meter. On Zapier you pay for the actions that reach out and touch another app. The thinking is free.

Scene 5 — Make credit (1.00×)

Make counts differently, and it is upfront about it. Make's help centre defines credits as the currency you buy and consume to use Make, and says each standard module action consumes one credit. Then the sentence that defines this whole comparison: an operation is a single module run to process data, or to check for new data. Checking is a chargeable event. And Make's documentation puts it beyond doubt: trigger modules use one credit per run regardless of whether they return data — a trigger that finds nothing still uses one credit for checking.

Scene 6 — Make bundles

And there is a multiplier, though it is narrower than most people think. Make's documentation says action modules — the add, update and delete steps — use as many credits as runs needed to process all bundles: one credit per input bundle processed. In plain English, a step that writes ten rows costs ten credits, not one. Iterators work the same way: one credit to split an array, plus one credit for every bundle sent downstream. This is the single biggest driver of surprise bills on Make, and it has nothing to do with the price of the plan. It is entirely about how many bundles each step has to process.

Scene 7 — Zapier ladder

Now the published prices. On Zapier's pricing page: a free plan with one hundred tasks a month. The Professional plan starts at twenty-nine ninety-nine a month billed monthly, or nineteen ninety-nine billed annually, and that entry rung includes seven hundred and fifty tasks. Team starts at one hundred and three fifty monthly, or sixty-nine annually, from two thousand tasks. Both plans climb a published ladder to one hundred thousand tasks a month — that is where Zapier's printed rungs stop — and Enterprise is custom. The plan name sets the features. The slider sets the volume.

Scene 8 — Make ladder (1.00×)

Make's ladder reads differently. Free gives up to one thousand credits a month. On Make's pricing page, Core is displayed at nine dollars, Pro at sixteen and Teams at twenty-nine — and here is the quirk. At the ten-thousand-credit setting, all three plans show that same ten thousand credits. You are not buying volume as you climb, you are buying features; volume comes from the slider. Make says annual billing saves fifteen percent or more but does not publish the exact figure, so we will not guess at one. And one flag we owe you: Zapier's own blog lists Make Core at twelve dollars, not nine. We went with Make's own page — a vendor's page beats a competitor's blog — but you may see a different number depending on your region, currency, and billing toggle.

Scene 9 — the rate, part 1

Now the comparison that actually means something: cost per unit, both at entry volume. Zapier Professional, billed monthly, is twenty-nine ninety-nine for seven hundred and fifty tasks. That is four cents per task. Make Core is displayed at nine dollars for ten thousand credits. That is nine hundredths of one cent per credit. Divide one by the other and a Zapier task costs about forty-four times what a Make credit costs. Two honest caveats on that. First, the number is ours, not theirs — it is arithmetic on two published figures, and you can check it in ten seconds.

Scene 10 — the rate, part 2

Second, Make's page does not make the billing basis of that nine dollars explicit, so we cannot tell you for certain it is the monthly-billed rate. If it turns out to be the annual-billed price, then on Make's own advertised fifteen percent saving the monthly equivalent is about ten dollars fifty-nine, and the ratio is nearer thirty-eight times than forty-four. Thirty-eight or forty-four, the direction of the answer does not move. Which one it is, does.

Scene 11 — the correction (1.00×)

Here is where most comparisons go wrong, including one we nearly published. It is tempting to hear that Make bills for empty checks and conclude Make is the expensive one. That gets it backwards. The faster meter is the thing working in Zapier's favour; Make's advantage is the price of the unit. So it is a race. Make counts more units. Make's units cost less. Which effect is bigger for your workflow?

Scene 12 — the 4 numbers (1.00×)

Four numbers, and you can read all four off one workflow. Number one: R, the runs per month that actually do something — real orders, real form fills. Number two: A, the actions per run that reach out and touch another app. Number three: C, the checks per month — if you poll every fifteen minutes that is about two thousand nine hundred; if your trigger is event-driven, C is just R. Number four: B, the extra bundles — items a module processes beyond the first.

Scene 13 — formulas + crossover

Two formulas. Zapier tasks equal R times A — because the trigger and the logic are free. Make credits equal C, plus R times A, plus B — because the checks count, the trigger counts, and the bundles multiply. Then multiply each by the rate you read off each pricing slider at your own volume. And the crossover rule, which is the whole video in one line: Zapier only wins on the meter when Make's unit count exceeds Zapier's by more than the price ratio. At entry tiers, that means more than forty-four times.

Scene 14 — shape 1

Shape one: low frequency, action heavy. A form submission that creates a contact, sends an email, updates a sheet and posts to Slack. Two hundred real submissions a month, event-driven trigger. Zapier: four actions times two hundred, eight hundred tasks. Make: the trigger plus four modules, five times two hundred, one thousand credits. Make counts one and a quarter times more units. Against a forty-four times price advantage, that is not close. On the meter, Make wins comfortably — and note Zapier's eight hundred tasks already pushes past the seven-fifty entry rung.

Scene 15 — shape 2

Shape two is the one built to hurt Make. Polling a mailbox every fifteen minutes, but real mail arriving only fifty times a month, three actions each. Zapier: polling is free, so a hundred and fifty tasks. Make: two thousand eight hundred and eighty checks, plus a hundred and fifty module runs, so three thousand and thirty credits. Make counts twenty times more units. That is a genuinely brutal ratio — and it still loses to forty-four. Even in the shape designed against it, Make's meter comes out cheaper.

Scene 16 — shape 3, part 1

Shape three: batch processing — the one where bundles are supposed to bite. A scheduled daily run pulls four hundred spreadsheet rows, filters them down to forty, and updates those forty. Zapier: the trigger and the filter are free, so forty tasks a day — one thousand two hundred a month. Make, on its published rules: one credit for the trigger check; one for the search step, because that step takes one bundle in even though four hundred come back; then forty credits, because the update step processes forty bundles. Forty-two a day. One thousand two hundred and sixty a month. One assumption there, and it is on screen: Make does not publish how it bills filters, so we model them at zero. So this is near parity — Make counts five percent more units, not eleven times more — and both sides sit inside their entry tiers. Bundles only multiply when a module actually has to process them. Now change that one thing.

Scene 17 — shape 3, part 2

Have the update run on all four hundred rows, and Make lands at twelve thousand and sixty credits a month, past the ten-thousand tier that Make publishes a price for. Above that the rate sits on a slider we could not read a quotable figure from, so we are not calling a winner there. That one you price off the slider yourself.

Scene 18 — traps

Four traps before you act on any of this. One: the forty-four times ratio is an entry-tier figure. It changes as you climb both sliders, so recompute at your own volume rather than borrowing ours. Two: Zapier publishes both monthly and annual prices; Make publishes a percentage but not the annual figure, so do not assume the discount. Three: on Make, a higher plan may buy features rather than volume. Four: Zapier states failed actions are not counted. We found no equivalent published promise from Make, so do not assume symmetry.

Scene 19 — limits (1.00×)

And the honest part: what a cost model cannot tell you. Whether a platform reliably connects to the specific apps your business runs on. The hours you will lose to a tool that does not fit how you think. Error recovery when a workflow breaks at two in the morning. How quickly support answers. None of that shows up on a pricing page — and for most small businesses, those factors decide the outcome long before the meter does.

Scene 20 — verdict

So, scoped strictly to the meter and strictly to prices published on the twenty-eighth of July, twenty twenty-six. Make's per-unit rate is roughly forty-four times lower at entry tiers, and in all three shapes we could price from published figures — all three kept deliberately inside the ten-thousand-credit tier Make actually quotes — that advantage outweighed Make's higher unit count, even in the polling-heavy case built against it. Where a shape ran past that tier, we stopped: Make publishes no readable rate up there, and we are not going to invent one. Zapier flips only in the extreme: very frequent checking with almost no real actions. Against that, Zapier's free logic steps and its published no-charge-on-failure rule make its bill easier to predict. Cheaper meter, Make. Fewer surprises, Zapier.
